# Half-marathon plan research for Stride

Reviewed 23 September 2026. Scope: half marathon only; the accepted marathon implementation is outside this change. Thirteen actual published plans were inspected across four publishers. These are coaching examples, not controlled evidence that one numerical schedule is optimal. Stride must author its own prescriptions rather than copy schedules. Source facts and proposed product rules are separated below.

## Synthesis

The common structure is an endurance outing, frequent comfortable running, and a workload appropriate to the existing runner. Published plans disagree on exact peak distance, taper duration, speed frequency and whether to use pace-based long finishes. That disagreement rules out presenting a single template's numbers as established science.

The useful consistent distinction is **durability plus experience**, not race distance alone. A novice can legitimately prepare with easy running. An experienced three-day runner can need larger easy outings without needing three hard days. An advanced runner may hold substantial weekly mileage rather than increase indefinitely. A faster runner's 120-minute outing and a slower runner's same-distance outing can have very different time cost.

## Proposed Stride engineering rules (original design decisions)

1. Preserve the declared opening weekly running distance and recent long run. Do not lift either to a publisher's opening template value. A disproportionate baseline, such as 30 km/week with a 20 km long run, is an input to redistribute later growth toward easy weekdays; it is not permission to inflate week one or shorten the accepted recent run.
2. Classify developing, established and advanced road profiles from consistent current running, weekly volume, long-run durability and workout background. Pace supplies dose duration; speed alone is not evidence of training tolerance. An advanced label must not be conferred solely by race-time prediction.
3. Use distinct ordinary-week roles: q0 means easy running plus an easy long run; q1 generally alternates controlled threshold and half-marathon-effort endurance as the block develops; q2 provides complementary weekday roles and keeps the long run easy by default. Explicit requested sessions are real controlled workouts, including finish-oriented plans. If time or recovery spacing cannot fit them, report the precise constraint instead of replacing them silently with strides.
4. Count strides separately from sustained quality and the endurance long run. A few accelerations must not satisfy an explicit threshold/interval-workout count. A quality long run, if offered later, must replace another demanding stimulus or reduce weekday dose; it must not add a hidden third workout.
5. Make quality work progression follow recent work minutes and repeat exposures, never the empty space in a daily ceiling. Missing history starts with short controlled blocks; it does not mean zero fitness or justify an advanced opening dose. Forecast exposures are planned progression, not fabricated completed training.
6. Author practical ceilings separately for warm-up, recoveries, main set and optional easy running. Bounded quality outings should typically fit roughly 30–75 minutes according to dose and ability; this is Stride policy, not a claimed universal physiological threshold. Do not turn a ten-minute main set into a two-hour workout to absorb weekly mileage. Redistribute remaining volume to suitable easy days or state that the selected days/caps cannot support it.
7. Treat long-run targets as ability-sensitive reference ranges, never mandatory floors: roughly 16–18 km for developing finish-focused profiles, 18–21 km for established profiles, and 20–24 km only with substantial existing durability. Preserve an already longer supported baseline and avoid forcing progress past useful endurance. Exact bounds require product tests and coaching review.
8. Progress in deliberate whole kilometres, with holds permitted when weekly/time capacity cannot fund the next kilometre. Use explicit recovery and taper phases for reductions. Increase weekday endurance rather than continually enlarging the long-run share. If the timeline cannot fund sensible growth, acknowledge limited preparation; do not compress missing weeks.
9. Resolve taper against days to race, not only calendar-week labels. A reasonable original default is ten to fourteen days of decreasing non-race running, with familiar short faster work retained. Compare training volume excluding the race. Ensure individual easy days do not inflate just because race-week total remains under an arbitrary weekly ceiling.
10. Preserve settings through regeneration, variety refresh, swapping and unit conversion. The validator must independently recalculate distance, quality count, step totals, recovery spacing and taper reductions; validating only precomputed metadata can reproduce the generator's mistakes.

## Verification cases implied by this research

- Baselines spanning 12/5, 20/8, 30/12, 30/20, 45/16, 65/20 and 90/24 km (weekly/long), with low-volume cases either conservatively supportable or explicitly limited by schedule, never silently inflated.
- Three through six running days; q0/q1/q2; finish/improve; gentle/balanced; zero/one/two recent workouts; unknown and explicitly reported recent work minutes.
- Fast and slow easy pace; time and distance workout formats; missing benchmark; unusually short and long blocks; midweek entry and each race weekday.
- First-week baseline equality, complete repetitions and recovery, independently counted weekday quality, bounded total quality-session time, no ordinary-week long-run regression, and race-excluded taper volume reduction.
- Identical marathon fixtures before and after this road-only change, plus custom/ultra isolation. The user's accepted marathon progression must not change as a side effect.

No publisher's full schedule is reproduced or used as a template. These observations inform independently authored policy and prescriptions.
